# Interview 11: Clinical director, six-vet small-animal hospital

Fictional interview for the Desk Test. Date: 2026-09-25. Interviewer: R. Okafor.

**Interviewer:** To begin, could you tell me about the clinic and your role?

**Participant:** I'm clinical director at a six-vet small-animal hospital in Ferncastle Rise. Thirty-four staff, nine of them part-time, plus a handful of relief vets. We signed up for Tidemark a year ago this month, on an annual plan.

**Interviewer:** What's working for you after a year?

**Participant:** The planning side is solid. I'm not the person who builds the rota, but I can see the whole hospital's week on one screen, which I never could before. That alone has made our surgical lists less chaotic.

**Interviewer:** Where does it struggle?

**Participant:** Approvals. Every swap that touches a vet comes to me, and I am, frankly, the wrong bottleneck. I get swap requests during consults, during surgery, on my day off. Last Christmas Eve I approved an on-call change from a supermarket queue because nobody else had permission to do it.

**Interviewer:** Can you delegate approvals?

**Participant:** Only by making someone a full admin, which gives them access to salaries and contracts as well. So I'm stuck choosing between being the only approver or handing out keys to everything. I want a deputy who can approve swaps and nothing else.

**Interviewer:** What happens when you don't get to an approval quickly?

**Participant:** People sort it out among themselves on a group chat and then nobody updates the app. So Tidemark says one thing, the chat says another, and the on-call vet trusts whichever she saw last. We had a Sunday where two vets each thought the other had the emergency phone.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned trust. Does the team trust what the schedule says?

**Participant:** Less than they should. When I make a change, the person affected sees the result but not my reasoning. A vet came into my office in April quite upset because she thought she'd been taken off orthopaedic lists as a judgement on her work. In fact I'd moved her to cover a colleague's surgery recovery. If the app had shown my reason, that conversation would have been a thank-you instead of a grievance.

**Interviewer:** Let's touch on pricing. How do you feel about it?

**Participant:** We pay $7 per seat on the annual deal, which is reasonable for the full-timers. My objection is the principle. A nurse doing two days a week costs us exactly what I cost, and the part-timers notice that we treat them as a line item.

**Interviewer:** The part-timers know about the pricing?

**Participant:** One of them saw the invoice on the printer. She asked, quite fairly, whether we'd thought about cutting part-time hours to save on software. We hadn't, but the question tells you how per-seat pricing reads from the outside.

**Interviewer:** If you were on Tidemark's product team, what would you prioritise?

**Participant:** Delegated approvals first, visible reasons second, and a fairer rate for part-time and relief staff third. Do those three and the app becomes something the whole team believes, not just the person who pays for it.
